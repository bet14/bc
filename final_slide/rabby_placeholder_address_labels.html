Fraud Detection Features

Address Poisoning & Contract Risk Labels

Two related defenses sit on the "who am I sending to / who is asking me to sign" side of Rabby: filtering poisoned-lookalike transfers out of transaction history, and surfacing plain-language risk labels — scam address, contract type, exchange-deposit mismatch — the moment an address is typed into a send flow.

In short
One defense cleans up what the sender already sees (history). The other warns the sender before a new address is even used (send flow). Both reuse the same backend signals and the same five-layer pipeline — just triggered earlier than a normal sign-request check.

1. Address Poisoning Defense

Address poisoning is an attack in which an adversary sends a tiny, often zero-value, transfer from a wallet address generated to visually resemble one a holder has genuinely transacted with before. The aim is not to move funds in that moment — it is to plant a lookalike entry inside the holder's transaction history, hoping a later careless copy-paste from that history sends real funds to the attacker's address instead of the intended one.

How it works

Backend flags the transferA backend check labels suspicious incoming transfers as scam before the wallet displays them.plain gloss: a "this looks fake" stamp added server-side, ahead of display
→

→
Copy button disappearsThe flagged row's address display loses its copy-to-clipboard icon — the exact click the attack is fishing for is no longer available.plain gloss: no copy icon on rows marked as scam
→
Same rule, every screenThe same scam check dims and tags entries consistently in the dashboard, the desktop view, and the grouping logic — one signal applied everywhere an address could be copied from.plain gloss: one flag, reused across every history surface

Why the copy icon matters most: dimming a row only helps a holder who is paying close attention. Removing the copy affordance removes the mechanical step the attack is actually betting on — one-click reuse of an address straight out of history, without the holder ever needing to notice the visual warning at all.
Independent research backs up why this filtering step matters: in their evaluation of 53 Ethereum wallets, Guan & Li (2025) specifically singled out Rabby for having "transfers flagged as scam transactions" — naming it as a distinguishing trait rather than a baseline expectation.
In short
A look-alike address gets sent into the sender's history hoping to be copied by accident later. Rabby's backend marks that entry as scam, the history view greys it out with a warning badge, and — the key step — removes its copy button so it physically cannot be reused with one click.
Why this is rare among wallets
Most wallets simply list every incoming and outgoing transfer exactly as the chain reports it, with no scam-likelihood judgment attached. That leaves the entire burden of spotting a poisoned lookalike on the holder's eyesight — precisely the weakness the attack is engineered to exploit. Filtering and tagging at the history layer, before any address is ever displayed as copyable, is the part most competing wallets skip.

2. Address & Contract Risk Labels

Separate from history filtering, Rabby evaluates the destination address itself the moment it is entered into a send flow — before any transaction is built — and renders a small set of plain-language labels describing what kind of address it is and why that might matter.

How it works

Address gets looked upThe moment a destination is typed, Rabby asks its backend what it knows about that address — contract details, scam flags, exchange identity.plain gloss: a quick background check on the address
→
Result becomes a short label listThat lookup is turned into a handful of plain-language labels rather than a wall of raw data.plain gloss: raw data → a few readable tags
→
Labels get ranked by seriousnessEach label has a fixed importance level, so the most worrying one always shows first — no matter what order the data arrived in.plain gloss: scariest label always shown on top
→
A confirmation screen sums it upA drawer shows the ranked labels plus the address type (personal wallet, multisig, exchange, contract) — a verdict, not a data dump.plain gloss: one clear screen — "here's what this address is, and why to be careful"

In short
Before sending, Rabby checks the destination address and turns whatever it finds into a short, ranked list of plain labels — "this looks like a scam," "this is a contract, not a wallet," "this exchange address won't accept deposits," and so on — so the sender sees a verdict rather than raw lookup data.
The five label types, in priority order (highest concern first)
LabelTrigger conditionWhat it tells the sender, in plain language
FORBIDDEN_TIPA server-side block on this specific sendThe backend has actively refused to let this transfer go through as described — the strongest stop signal the system can issue, ranked above every other label.
SCAM_ADDRESSis_danger or is_scam on the address profileShown as: scam address warning — this destination has already been flagged as dangerous or implicated in scams by Rabby's backend.
CONTRACT_ADDRESSAddress resolves to a contract, and is not a recognized multisigShown as: this is a contract, not a personal wallet — sending ordinary transfers to a contract address can mean funds land somewhere never meant to receive them directly.
CEX_NO_DEPOSITAddress belongs to a known exchange but is not a valid deposit addressShown as: this exchange address does not accept deposits — sending to it risks funds the exchange will not credit to any account.
NEVER_SENDNo prior outgoing transfer from this account to this addressShown as: first time sending here — a softer, informational nudge rather than a danger signal: simply "double-check this one, it's new."
Why ranking matters as much as detection: a single address can trigger more than one label at once — for example, a contract that is also flagged as dangerous. sortRisksDesc guarantees the sender's eye lands on scam address before this is a contract, so attention goes to the label that should actually change the decision, not whichever one happened to load first.

Contract reputation, reused from the rule engine

When the destination resolves to a contract, the same reputation checks the rule engine runs for token approvals are available to describe it here too: whether it is an externally-owned account masquerading as a contract destination (rule 1022), whether it was deployed less than three days ago (rule 1024), whether Rabby has independently flagged it (rule 1029), and whether it carries zero measurable trust value (rule 1150). The result is that a "this is a contract" label is never just that bare fact — it travels with the same age, verification, and reputation context a holder would otherwise only see deep inside an approval screen.

Whitelist guard as the override layer

An address the sender has explicitly placed on a personal whitelist is treated differently: Rabby renders a distinct "whitelist guard" indicator instead of running it through the same alarm path as an unknown destination. This is the same Layer-4 idea used throughout the engine — a holder's own, on-device judgment can soften (never silently remove) what the automated layers would otherwise surface.

3. How It Works Maps Onto the Five-Layer Architecture

Both features above are not separate systems bolted onto Rabby — they are two passes of the same five-layer pipeline that evaluates every signature request, just triggered earlier: at the moment an address is typed or a history list renders, rather than at the moment a transaction is about to be signed.

In short
Same five steps every time: get the facts (backend), shape them (analysis), turn them into ranked labels (rules), let personal whitelists soften them (local data), and show the verdict (UI). These two features just run that pipeline at "address typed" or "history loaded" instead of "about to sign."

What each layer contributes to these two features specifically

Layer 1: Backend Intelligence
Supplies: the raw judgment calls neither the wallet nor the chain can make alone
• plain gloss: a scam stamp on each transfer record
• plain gloss: a profile of the address — contract or not, dangerous or not, how old, how trusted
• plain gloss: is this a known exchange address?
Layer 2: Action Analysis
Supplies: the typed, structured shape the next layer needs to reason over
• plain gloss: tidies the raw profile into the shape the rule step needs (is it a contract? dangerous? exchange-deposit ok? sent here before?)
• plain gloss: for history rows, just passes the scam stamp along — nothing more to figure out
Layer 3: Rule Engine
Supplies: the named, ranked verdicts a sender actually reads
• plain gloss: turns raw flags into a ranked, plain-language label list — blocked → scam → contract → exchange-deposit issue → first-time send
• plain gloss: for contracts, the same checks used on approval screens (how old, verified, flagged, trusted) add context to the "this is a contract" label
Layer 4: Local User Data
Supplies: the sender's own, on-device judgment as an override
• plain gloss: an address the sender already trusts gets a calm "whitelist guard" mark instead of an alarm
• plain gloss: personal trust can soften a warning — it never makes one disappear silently
Layer 5: UI Layer
Supplies: the surfaces the sender actually sees and acts on
• plain gloss: in history — a "Scam" badge, a greyed-out row, no copy button
• plain gloss: in the send flow — a confirmation drawer with the ranked labels and an address-type summary, the final plain verdict before funds move
Scenario: a poisoned lookalike sits in the sender's history

A sender opens their transaction list to copy a contact's address from a transfer made three weeks ago. Sitting just above it is a near-zero transfer from an address differing by two characters — planted to be copied by mistake.

L1 — already judged itThe planted transfer was evaluated when history synced and came back marked as scam.
→
L2 — passes the mark alongThat mark travels straight into the row's display data — nothing new to work out.
→
L3 — no fresh ruling neededThe backend's scam call already is the verdict for this row.
→
L4 — whitelist doesn't applyIt would only soften things if the sender had whitelisted this exact address — impossible, since it was freshly made for this attack.
→
L5 — shows it, disarms itRow appears greyed out with a "Scam" badge and no copy button. Visible for transparency, but not reusable. The real contact's row just below stays full-opacity with its copy button intact.

→ The defense wins not by hiding the attack, but by removing the one mechanical action — the copy click — the whole attack depends on.
